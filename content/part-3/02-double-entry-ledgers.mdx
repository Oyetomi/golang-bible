## Debits and Credits

Let me set the scene with a concrete scenario — because abstract accounting jargon is how you end up confused and I end up disappointed.

**The scenario:** You're building the ledger for Meridian, a payments platform. Alice has a `checking` account with $200 in it. She sends $50 to Bob. How do you record that without creating or destroying any money?

This is exactly the problem double-entry accounting solves. Let's build up to the answer one idea at a time.

<Define term="Double-entry accounting">
**Double-entry** means every financial event is recorded as at least two entries that exactly cancel each other out. When Alice sends Bob $50, one entry subtracts $50 from Alice and another entry adds $50 to Bob — the two together sum to zero. Nothing is created; nothing is destroyed. The system catches unbalanced postings the instant they're written, not weeks later when a discrepancy shows up in a report. **This is why ledgers don't lose money silently.**
</Define>

<Define term="Debit">
A **debit** records value flowing *into* an asset or expense account (or *out of* a liability or equity account). In our convention: debit entries carry a positive signed value. When Bob receives $50, his checking account — an asset — is debited: `+5000 cents`. Think of it as the account being "loaded."
</Define>

<Define term="Credit">
A **credit** records value flowing *out of* an asset or expense account (or *into* a liability or equity account). In our convention: credit entries carry a negative signed value. When Alice sends $50, her checking account is credited: `-5000 cents`. Think of it as the account being "unloaded."
</Define>

**On the job:** In production, a single-sided entry that persists to the database will corrupt account balances, trigger false fraud alerts, and create phantom discrepancies that take hours of manual reconciliation to diagnose — and sometimes never get explained. Enforcing the zero-sum invariant at the constructor level means this class of bug is caught before the first database round-trip.

### Every account is a bucket

An **account** is a named bucket that tracks the flow of value. In Meridian's ledger, examples include:

- `alice:checking` — Alice's checking balance
- `bob:checking` — Bob's checking balance
- `platform:revenue` — The platform's fee income
- `platform:liability:user-funds` — Money the platform holds on behalf of users

Every movement of value touches **at least two** accounts. That's the fundamental rule. When Alice pays Bob $50, money leaves one bucket and enters another. We call each touch an **entry**.

### Debits and credits — defined for engineers

These words carry enormous historical baggage (generations of accounting students have memorized them with flashcards). I'm going to define them operationally so they're actually useful:

  <ConceptCard label="Credit">A credit records value **flowing out of** an asset account, or **flowing into** a liability/equity account. Latin for "he trusts." In Go: `EntryCredit`.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Asset account">Things the entity *owns* or is *owed* — cash, receivables. A debit **increases** an asset account. A credit **decreases** it.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Liability account">Things the entity *owes* others — deposits held, loans payable. A credit **increases** a liability. A debit **decreases** it.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Equity account">The owner's residual claim (assets minus liabilities). Behaves like a liability for debit/credit purposes.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Revenue / Expense">Revenue: credits increase it (earning more). Expenses: debits increase them (spending more).</ConceptCard>
  <Gotcha>The debit-increases-assets / credit-increases-liabilities rule trips up every engineer on first contact. The shortcut: think of debits and credits as **directions on a number line**, not as "add" and "subtract." The direction's effect depends on the account type. We'll come back to this — your Go model won't rely on knowing this by heart, because the invariant does the job instead.</Gotcha>
</ConceptGrid>

### The accounting equation — for engineers, no MBA required

<Define term="The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity)">
Every double-entry system is built on one identity that must hold at all times: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**. Translated for engineers: everything the entity *has* (assets) equals everything it *owes* (liabilities) plus everything the owners *claim* (equity). It is a conservation law — like conservation of energy, but for money. Any transaction that preserves this identity can be verified mechanically, which is exactly what we will do in Go.
</Define>

Here's how it applies to Meridian's Alice→Bob transfer:

| Account | Type | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| `alice:checking` | Asset (Alice's) | Decreases by $50 — credit |
| `bob:checking` | Asset (Bob's) | Increases by $50 — debit |

Two entries. Two accounts touched. And critically: the **sum is zero** — one entry takes $50 away, the other adds $50 back. No money created. No money destroyed. The books balance.

## The Balance Invariant

The beating heart of double-entry is one mathematical rule that must be true at every instant, not just at month-end:

<Define term="The balance invariant (entries sum to zero)">
**For every transaction, the signed sum of all entry amounts is zero.** Debits are positive, credits are negative (by our convention). A $50 transfer from Alice to Bob produces two entries: +5000 and −5000. Their sum is 0. This is not accounting policy — it is a conservation law. It means value cannot appear from nowhere and cannot vanish into a void. Enforcing this at the Go constructor level means an unbalanced transaction is a compile-time-detectable error, not a silent database bug.
</Define>

Why does this matter for engineers?

1. **Self-checking:** If you record a $50 credit but forget the matching debit, the sum is -$50, not 0. The invariant fails. You catch the bug *before* it hits the database.
2. **Audit trail:** Given any set of ledger entries, you can verify their correctness by summing them. No external oracle needed — the data checks itself.
3. **No "orphan" money:** A naive system where you `UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 50 WHERE id = alice` can crash between the debit and credit, leaving Alice $50 poorer with Bob none the richer. Double-entry records both sides atomically or not at all.

**On the job:** At scale, without the balance invariant enforced at write time, a single crashed service between two `UPDATE` calls creates a discrepancy that compounds silently for days. The reconciliation engineer finds it at month-end — if they're lucky.

<UnderTheHood title="Why enforcing the invariant at the constructor boundary makes a class of bugs impossible">

In most systems, the balance invariant is enforced by a mix of application code, database constraints, and hope. If you have a function `postEntries(entries []Entry)` that just inserts rows, a caller can accidentally pass only one side of a transfer. The database doesn't know about double-entry; it will happily store the unbalanced entries. The bug surfaces hours later as a mysterious discrepancy in an account balance.

In Go, we can do better: enforce the invariant at the **construction site** of a `Transaction`. The only way to create a valid `Transaction` is through a constructor that *rejects* unbalanced input with an error. There is **no way** to construct a `Transaction` with entries that don't sum to zero — not through a bug, not through a forgotten check, not through a code path you didn't test.

This is the Go proverb "make illegal states unrepresentable" in its purest fintech form. The compiler doesn't know about accounting, but the constructor does. And once the constructor runs, the type itself carries the proof of validity.

As of Go 1.26, there's nothing magical happening — this is just idiomatic constructor validation. The impact at scale is enormous: a class of bugs that would require careful code review to catch is instead caught at the boundary where the data enters the system, every time, automatically.

</UnderTheHood>

## Append-Only Immutability

Here's the part that makes every SQL engineer uncomfortable the first time they hear it.

**Ledger entries are never updated. Ledger entries are never deleted.**

Not "try not to update them." Not "only update them in emergencies." Never. Full stop.

<Define term="Append-only / immutable ledger">
An **append-only ledger** is one where entries can only be added, never modified or removed. Every entry is a fact about the past — "$50 left Alice's account at 14:03:22 UTC on June 3rd" — and facts about the past do not change. If an error must be corrected, you post a **new** entry that undoes the old one. The original is never touched. This gives you a tamper-evident history: an auditor can scan every entry from genesis to now and verify that nothing was silently altered.
</Define>

### Why immutability matters

The ledger is not a scoreboard showing current values — it is a **complete history of every event that ever happened**. Every entry is a fact about the past, and facts about the past do not change. $50 left Alice's account on June 3rd. That happened. You cannot un-happen it.

This matters for three reasons engineers care about:

1. **Audit.** A regulator, an auditor, or your own on-call engineer can look at the ledger and reconstruct the state of every account at any point in time. If entries could be edited, that history would be corrupted — you'd have no way to know whether a balance today reflects legitimate transactions or a backdated edit.
2. **Correctness.** Derived balances (computed by summing entries) are always reproducible. You can recompute any account's balance from scratch and get the same answer. If entries were mutable, two replicas might compute different answers.
3. **Concurrency safety.** Immutable data has no write conflicts. Multiple readers can scan the ledger simultaneously without any locking on the entries themselves (though inserting a new transaction still requires a careful write — covered in the next chapter, Transactions, Consistency & Sagas).

**On the job:** Regulators under PCI-DSS and SOX require a tamper-evident audit trail. A ledger where rows can be `UPDATE`d silently fails that requirement the moment any engineer touches a row directly. Append-only is not gold-plating — it's the minimum bar for a compliant fintech system.

### Corrections are new entries

<Define term="A reversal / compensating entry">
A **reversal** (also called a compensating entry or compensating transaction) is a new transaction that exactly undoes a prior transaction. Every entry in the original is replicated with its debit/credit direction flipped, producing a new transaction that also sums to zero. The original is preserved in the log; the reversal sits next to it, timestamped, auditable. This is the only correct way to correct a mistake in an immutable ledger.
</Define>

So what happens when someone makes a mistake? Say a $50 transfer was posted to the wrong account. In a mutable system, you'd `UPDATE` the entry. In Meridian's immutable ledger, you:

1. Post a **reversal** (a compensating entry that exactly undoes the original).
2. Post the **correction** (a new transaction with the right accounts).

<Callout kind="pro" title="The ledger as an event log">

If this sounds like event sourcing — it is. A double-entry ledger is one of the oldest event-sourced systems in existence. Each entry is an **immutable event** that happened at a specific time. The current state of any account (its balance) is a **projection** derived by replaying those events. This is why ledger systems map so naturally onto modern event-driven architecture; the Event-Driven Architecture & Kafka chapter covers the connection in full.

## Modeling a Ledger in Go with Enforced Invariants

Now we build it. Everything you've learned about the invariant, the immutability, and the account model — it all goes into a Go type system that makes the wrong thing **impossible to compile**.

### The Money type

We're using integer cents from the previous chapter, Representing Money — Never Use Floats. If you haven't read it, the rule is: **money is never a float**. Always integer minor units. On a 64-bit platform, `int64` holds up to ~92 quadrillion cents — more than enough for any realistic ledger balance.

```go
// AmountCents is a signed count of the currency's minor unit (cents for USD).
// Positive = debit, negative = credit — by our convention.
// This convention must be consistent across the whole system.
type AmountCents int64
```

### The Entry type

An `Entry` is one leg of a transaction — one account touched, with one amount:

```go
// EntryType distinguishes debits from credits explicitly.
type EntryType int

const (
	EntryDebit  EntryType = 1
	EntryCredit EntryType = -1
)

// Entry is one leg of a double-entry transaction.
// It records which account was touched, the direction, and the amount.
type Entry struct {
	AccountID string
	Type      EntryType
	// Amount is always a positive number of cents.
	// The signed value used in invariant checking is Amount * int64(Type).
	Amount AmountCents
}

// SignedCents returns the amount with sign applied: positive for debits,
// negative for credits. This is the value used when summing the transaction.
func (e Entry) SignedCents() int64 {
	return int64(e.Amount) * int64(e.Type)
}
```

### The Transaction type and its constructor

Here's the key insight: the `Transaction` struct has **unexported fields only**. The only way to create a valid `Transaction` is through `NewTransaction`, which enforces the balance invariant. If you pass unbalanced entries, you get an error — not a panic, not a silent bug, an explicit `error` that forces the caller to deal with it.

```go
// Transaction is a balanced, immutable set of ledger entries.
// It can only be constructed via NewTransaction, which enforces
// the balance invariant: the signed sum of all entries is zero.
type Transaction struct {
	id      string
	entries []Entry
	// postedAt is set by the ledger on commit, not by the caller.
}

// NewTransaction constructs a Transaction and rejects it if the entries
// do not sum to zero. A Transaction with fewer than two entries is
// structurally invalid and is also rejected.
func NewTransaction(id string, entries []Entry) (Transaction, error) {
	if len(entries) < 2 {
		return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
			"transaction %q: need at least 2 entries, got %d",
			id, len(entries),
		)
	}

	var sum int64
	for _, e := range entries {
		if e.Amount <= 0 {
			return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
				"transaction %q: entry amount must be positive, got %d",
				id, e.Amount,
			)
		}
		sum += e.SignedCents()
	}

	if sum != 0 {
		return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
			"transaction %q: entries do not balance (sum=%d cents, want 0)",
			id, sum,
		)
	}

	// Deep-copy the slice so callers can't mutate our entries after construction.
	cp := make([]Entry, len(entries))
	copy(cp, entries)
	return Transaction{id: id, entries: cp}, nil
}

// ID returns the transaction's identifier.
func (t Transaction) ID() string { return t.id }

// Entries returns a copy of the entries — entries are immutable
// once the transaction is constructed.
func (t Transaction) Entries() []Entry {
	cp := make([]Entry, len(t.entries))
	copy(cp, t.entries)
	return cp
}
```

### The Ledger type

The `Ledger` is an append-only store of committed transactions. It never removes or modifies a posted transaction:

```go
// Ledger is an in-memory append-only store of Transactions.
// In production this backs onto a database; the interface is identical.
type Ledger struct {
	mu           sync.RWMutex
	transactions []Transaction
}

// Post commits a balanced Transaction to the ledger.
// Once posted, the transaction cannot be modified or removed.
func (l *Ledger) Post(tx Transaction) {
	l.mu.Lock()
	defer l.mu.Unlock()
	l.transactions = append(l.transactions, tx)
}

// BalanceCents returns the current balance for accountID by summing
// all entries for that account across all posted transactions.
// This is the event-sourced / derived balance approach.
func (l *Ledger) BalanceCents(accountID string) int64 {
	l.mu.RLock()
	defer l.mu.RUnlock()

	var balance int64
	for _, tx := range l.transactions {
		for _, e := range tx.entries {
			if e.AccountID == accountID {
				balance += e.SignedCents()
			}
		}
	}
	return balance
}
```

package main

import (
	"fmt"
	"sync"
)

// AmountCents is a signed count of the currency's minor unit.
type AmountCents int64

// EntryType distinguishes debits (+1) from credits (-1).
type EntryType int

const (
	EntryDebit  EntryType = 1
	EntryCredit EntryType = -1
)

// Entry is one leg of a double-entry transaction.
type Entry struct {
	AccountID string
	Type      EntryType
	Amount    AmountCents // always positive; sign comes from Type
}

// SignedCents returns the signed value: positive for debits, negative for credits.
func (e Entry) SignedCents() int64 {
	return int64(e.Amount) * int64(e.Type)
}

// Transaction is a balanced, immutable set of ledger entries.
type Transaction struct {
	id      string
	entries []Entry
}

// NewTransaction constructs a Transaction, rejecting unbalanced entries.
func NewTransaction(id string, entries []Entry) (Transaction, error) {
	if len(entries) < 2 {
		return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
			"transaction %q: need at least 2 entries, got %d",
			id, len(entries),
		)
	}
	var sum int64
	for _, e := range entries {
		if e.Amount <= 0 {
			return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
				"transaction %q: entry amount must be positive, got %d",
				id, e.Amount,
			)
		}
		sum += e.SignedCents()
	}
	if sum != 0 {
		return Transaction{}, fmt.Errorf(
			"transaction %q: entries do not balance (sum=%d cents, want 0)",
			id, sum,
		)
	}
	cp := make([]Entry, len(entries))
	copy(cp, entries)
	return Transaction{id: id, entries: cp}, nil
}

// ID returns the transaction identifier.
func (t Transaction) ID() string { return t.id }

// Entries returns an immutable copy of the entries.
func (t Transaction) Entries() []Entry {
	cp := make([]Entry, len(t.entries))
	copy(cp, t.entries)
	return cp
}

// Ledger is an append-only store of Transactions.
type Ledger struct {
	mu           sync.RWMutex
	transactions []Transaction
}

// Post commits a balanced Transaction to the ledger.
func (l *Ledger) Post(tx Transaction) {
	l.mu.Lock()
	defer l.mu.Unlock()
	l.transactions = append(l.transactions, tx)
}

// BalanceCents returns the event-sourced balance for accountID in cents.
func (l *Ledger) BalanceCents(accountID string) int64 {
	l.mu.RLock()
	defer l.mu.RUnlock()
	var balance int64
	for _, tx := range l.transactions {
		for _, e := range tx.entries {
			if e.AccountID == accountID {
				balance += e.SignedCents()
			}
		}
	}
	return balance
}

func main() {
	ledger := &Ledger{}

	// Seed Alice with $200 by crediting the funding source and debiting her account.
	fund, err := NewTransaction("txn-fund-alice", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 20000},
		{AccountID: "platform:equity", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 20000},
	})
	if err != nil {
		panic(err)
	}
	ledger.Post(fund)
	fmt.Printf("After funding: alice=%d cents, platform:equity=%d cents\n",
		ledger.BalanceCents("alice:checking"),
		ledger.BalanceCents("platform:equity"))

	// Transfer $50 from Alice to Bob.
	transfer, err := NewTransaction("txn-001", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 5000},
		{AccountID: "bob:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 5000},
	})
	if err != nil {
		panic(err)
	}
	ledger.Post(transfer)
	fmt.Printf("After transfer: alice=%d cents, bob=%d cents\n",
		ledger.BalanceCents("alice:checking"),
		ledger.BalanceCents("bob:checking"))

	// Attempt an unbalanced transaction — the constructor rejects it.
	_, err = NewTransaction("txn-bad", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 5000},
		// Missing the matching debit — sum will be -5000, not 0.
	})
	if err != nil {
		fmt.Println("Correctly rejected:", err)
	}

	// Reversal: undo the transfer (a refund to Alice from Bob).
	reversal, err := NewTransaction("txn-001-reversal", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "bob:checking", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 5000},
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 5000},
	})
	if err != nil {
		panic(err)
	}
	ledger.Post(reversal)
	fmt.Printf("After reversal: alice=%d cents, bob=%d cents\n",
		ledger.BalanceCents("alice:checking"),
		ledger.BalanceCents("bob:checking"))
}

### Testing the invariant — table-driven and fuzz

// --- fuzz test: any randomly generated balanced pair of entries must sum to zero ---

func FuzzNewTransaction_BalancedEntriesAlwaysSumToZero(f *testing.F) {
	// Seed the fuzzer with some valid amounts.
	f.Add(int64(100), int64(200), int64(300))
	f.Add(int64(1), int64(99999), int64(50000))

	f.Fuzz(func(t *testing.T, a, b, c int64) {
		// Clamp to positive values to satisfy the Amount > 0 invariant.
		if a <= 0 {
			a = 1
		}
		if b <= 0 {
			b = 1
		}
		// Build a provably balanced three-leg transaction:
		// credit a+b from source, debit a to dest1, debit b to dest2.
		total := a + b
		entries := []Entry{
			{AccountID: "source", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: AmountCents(total)},
			{AccountID: "dest1", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: AmountCents(a)},
			{AccountID: "dest2", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: AmountCents(b)},
		}

		// This must always succeed — it's provably balanced by construction.
		txn, err := NewTransaction(fmt.Sprintf("fuzz-%d-%d", a, b), entries)
		if err != nil {
			t.Fatalf("balanced transaction rejected: %v (a=%d b=%d)", err, a, b)
		}

		// Verify the sum ourselves.
		var sum int64
		for _, e := range txn.Entries() {
			sum += e.SignedCents()
		}
		if sum != 0 {
			t.Fatalf("sum of balanced transaction entries is %d, want 0", sum)
		}

		// c is an unused fuzz parameter left here to give the fuzzer a third
		// axis of variation across runs.
		_ = c
	})
}

## Computing Balances

Now that we have an append-only ledger full of entries, how do we answer the most fundamental question: **what is Alice's balance right now?**

Two strategies, two trade-offs.

### Strategy 1 — Derived (event-sourced) balance

Sum all entries for the account from the beginning of time:

```go
func (l *Ledger) BalanceCents(accountID string) int64 {
	l.mu.RLock()
	defer l.mu.RUnlock()
	var balance int64
	for _, tx := range l.transactions {
		for _, e := range tx.entries {
			if e.AccountID == accountID {
				balance += e.SignedCents()
			}
		}
	}
	return balance
}
```

**Advantages:**
- The balance is always correct — it's computed from the authoritative source of truth.
- No denormalization to get out of sync.
- You can compute the balance at **any point in time** by filtering entries by timestamp.

**Disadvantages:**
- For an account with millions of entries, this is O(n) per call. At high transaction volume, this becomes slow.
- In a production database, you'd add an index on `(account_id, posted_at)` and query only the relevant partition — but it's still a full scan of that account's history.

### Strategy 2 — Cached (materialized) balance

Store a running total alongside the entries. On each new transaction, update the cache atomically in the same database transaction:

```go
// In production: the balance column lives in an accounts table.
// It is updated in the SAME database transaction that inserts entries.
// If the transaction rolls back, both the entries AND the balance update
// are rolled back together — they are always consistent.

// Pseudocode for the database transaction:
//   BEGIN;
//   INSERT INTO ledger_entries (...) VALUES (...), (...);
//   UPDATE accounts SET balance_cents = balance_cents + $delta
//     WHERE id = $account_id;
//   COMMIT;
```

**Advantages:**
- O(1) balance reads — just read the `balance_cents` column.
- Essential at high transaction volume.

**Disadvantages:**
- The cached balance and the derived balance can become inconsistent if there's a bug in your write path.
- You need to verify the cache periodically by recomputing from entries (a **reconciliation job** — covered in the Auditability & Compliance chapter).

**On the job:** Production ledgers at Meridian-scale use cached balances for fast reads and run a nightly reconciliation job that compares cached totals to derived sums. Any discrepancy is a P1 alert — it means a write-path bug persisted an unbalanced state.

<ConceptGrid cols={2}>
  <ConceptCard label="Derived balance">Always correct. O(n) cost. Use for low-volume accounts, historical queries, and audit verification.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Cached balance">O(1) cost. Can drift. Use for high-volume accounts when you also run periodic reconciliation to catch discrepancies.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Reconciliation">A scheduled job that recomputes derived balances and compares them to cached balances. Discrepancies are alerts. This is the safety net, not the fix.</ConceptCard>
  <Gotcha>Never cache a balance and skip reconciliation. The cache is a read optimization, not the source of truth. The ledger entries are always the source of truth. If they disagree, the entries win.</Gotcha>
</ConceptGrid>

<UnderTheHood title="Why the sum-equals-zero invariant is the whole game">

Consider what happens at scale without the constructor invariant. You have 10 engineers, 40 services, and a ledger that's grown over three years. Service A posts a debit. Service B crashes before posting the matching credit. The database has an unbalanced entry. Nobody knows — not the database, not the application, not the monitoring. The discrepancy compounds over days.

With the constructor invariant enforced at the type boundary:

1. **Service A cannot post an entry without the matching credit.** `NewTransaction` returns an error if the entries don't sum to zero. The caller must handle that error — there is no way to silently swallow it without the Go compiler making you write `_` (which is a code smell so obvious it gets caught in review).
2. **The database can verify the invariant independently** with a SQL check constraint or a periodic audit query: `SELECT txn_id, SUM(signed_cents) FROM ledger_entries GROUP BY txn_id HAVING SUM(signed_cents) != 0`. This query should return zero rows — forever.
3. **Reconciliation becomes fast:** you don't need to compare every entry; you can compare the derived sum against the cached balance and verify the global sum (total assets = total liabilities + equity) against zero.

The invariant is not a nice-to-have. It's the load-bearing wall. As of Go 1.26, the language has no magic to make this automatic — but the constructor pattern makes it as close to automatic as a dynamically-enforced system can get. You pay for it once, in one constructor, and the invariant holds across every caller for the lifetime of the system.

</UnderTheHood>

package main

import "fmt"

func Reverse(original Transaction, newID string) (Transaction, error) {
	entries := original.Entries()
	reversed := make([]Entry, len(entries))
	for i, e := range entries {
		flipped := EntryDebit
		if e.Type == EntryDebit {
			flipped = EntryCredit
		}
		reversed[i] = Entry{
			AccountID: e.AccountID,
			Type:      flipped,
			Amount:    e.Amount,
		}

	return NewTransaction(newID, reversed)
}

		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 5000},
		{AccountID: "bob:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 5000},

import "fmt"

// BalanceAtCents returns the balance considering only the
// first n transactions (n=1 means after the first transaction).
func (l *Ledger) BalanceAtCents(accountID string, n int) int64 {

	for _, tx := range l.transactions[:limit] {
		for _, e := range tx.entries {
			if e.AccountID == accountID {
				balance += e.SignedCents()
			}
		}
	}
	return balance
}

<Exercise n={5} title="Global invariant check" level="hard">

Write a function `AuditGlobalBalance(l *Ledger) error` that verifies the global double-entry invariant: the sum of **all** signed entry values across all posted transactions in the ledger must equal zero. <Solution>

```go
package main

import (
	"fmt"
)

// AuditGlobalBalance checks the global double-entry invariant:
// the sum of all signed entry amounts across all transactions must be zero.
// A non-zero sum means money was created or destroyed — a critical ledger error.
func AuditGlobalBalance(l *Ledger) error {
	l.mu.RLock()
	defer l.mu.RUnlock()

	var globalSum int64
	for _, tx := range l.transactions {
		for _, e := range tx.entries {
			globalSum += e.SignedCents()
		}
	}
	if globalSum != 0 {
		return fmt.Errorf(
			"global ledger invariant violated: sum of all entries is %d cents, want 0",
			globalSum,
		)
	}
	return nil
}

func main() {
	ledger := &Ledger{}

	fund, _ := NewTransaction("txn-fund", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 20000},
		{AccountID: "platform:equity", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 20000},
	})
	ledger.Post(fund)

	transfer, _ := NewTransaction("txn-transfer", []Entry{
		{AccountID: "alice:checking", Type: EntryCredit, Amount: 5000},
		{AccountID: "bob:checking", Type: EntryDebit, Amount: 5000},
	})
	ledger.Post(transfer)

	if err := AuditGlobalBalance(ledger); err != nil {
		fmt.Println("AUDIT FAILED:", err)
	} else {
		fmt.Println("Audit passed: global ledger is balanced.")
	}
}
```

<Recap>

- **Double-entry** means every transaction touches at least two accounts. Every debit has a matching credit. No money is created or destroyed — the accounting equation `Assets = Liabilities + Equity` holds at all times.
- **The balance invariant** is the load-bearing rule: for every transaction, the sum of all signed entry amounts is zero. Enforcing this at the `NewTransaction` constructor boundary makes illegal states unrepresentable — the constructor guarantees it on every code path.
- **Append-only immutability** means entries are never updated or deleted. A correction is a new compensating (reversal) transaction followed by a new correction transaction. The ledger is a complete, tamper-evident history of every event.
- **The Go model**: `Entry` with `SignedCents()`, `Transaction` with unexported fields and a validating constructor, `Ledger` as an append-only store with a read-write mutex. Money is always integer cents — never a float.
- **Derived vs. cached balances**: summing entries is always correct but O(n); a cached balance column is O(1) but requires periodic reconciliation to catch drift. Both strategies have a place; neither replaces the other.
- **Testing**: table-driven tests verify the constructor's rejection logic; fuzz tests (stable since Go 1.18, improved corpus tooling in Go 1.26) verify that any correctly balanced set of entries satisfies the invariant under random input. Both are mandatory in fintech.
