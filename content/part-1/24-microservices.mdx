## Monolith vs Microservices

Here's the scene: you and two engineers have been building **Meridian**, a payments and ledger backend. One binary. One Postgres. One deploy. It handles user accounts, payment processing, and email notifications. Traffic is growing; the team wants to move fast without stepping on each other.

Someone says the magic word: *microservices*.

Before you do anything, understand what you're actually choosing between.

<Define term="Monolith vs. Microservices">
A **monolith** is a single deployable binary — all domains (accounts, payments, notifications) compiled and deployed together. Service calls are function calls: zero network, zero serialization, compiler-checked types. A **microservices** architecture splits those domains into separate binaries that communicate over a network. That network hop changes the failure model, the latency, and the operational complexity. Neither is inherently better — the question is whether the benefits of independence justify the cost of distribution for *your* team *right now*. For most teams, it doesn't, yet.
</Define>

### The modular monolith — the architecture nobody talks about

A **monolith** is a single deployable unit. That doesn't mean spaghetti. A well-structured monolith is divided into clean **packages** with enforced dependency directions — it just happens to compile into one binary and run as one process.

```go
// Meridian monolith — three packages, one binary
//
// meridian/
//   cmd/server/main.go        ← entry point
//   accounts/service.go       ← Account domain logic
//   payments/service.go       ← Payment + ledger domain logic
//   notifications/service.go  ← Notification domain logic
```

Inside one process, calling across packages is a **function call**. Nanoseconds. No network. No serialization. No partial failure. The compiler checks your types at every boundary. Rollback is one `git revert` and a redeploy.

This is what you built in the Blog Aggregator project (Part 1). And it is *good*.

**Microservices** split those packages into separate binaries that communicate over a network. That network hop changes everything — and we'll be very precise about what changes and why.

### Conway's Law: your architecture mirrors your org chart

In 1967, Mel Conway observed that organizations build systems that mirror their communication structures. If you have one team, you'll build one system. If you have three teams, the system will naturally cleave into three pieces — because the teams can't coordinate closely enough to do otherwise.

This is a *description*, not a prescription. **Don't split into microservices to "enable team autonomy" if you don't actually have multiple autonomous teams.** You'll pay the operational cost of microservices while your two engineers are still Slacking each other about shared DB schemas.

### When a split is actually justified

You should consider splitting when **at least two** of these are true:

  <ConceptCard label="Genuine team autonomy">Multiple independent teams exist, and deploy coupling is measurably slowing them down — not hypothetically, *measurably*.</ConceptCard>

## Service Boundaries

Suppose the pain is real and a split is warranted. Where do you cut?

This is the most consequential technical decision in a microservices architecture, and it's the one most often rushed. A bad cut produces a **distributed monolith** — the worst of both worlds.

### Bounded contexts and domain-driven design

<Define term="Bounded Context / Service Boundary" since="Software Design & Architecture (Part 1)">
A **bounded context** is a subsystem where one domain model is internally consistent and authoritative. Inside the context, the model is coherent; outside it, the same concept (e.g. "account") may have a different shape. A **service boundary** is where you draw the cut: the bounded context becomes a separate deployable. Cut in the wrong place and two services become inseparable — you own a distributed monolith. Cut correctly and each service can be deployed, scaled, and failed independently. The concept comes from Domain-Driven Design (DDD); you don't need to read the whole book to apply the lens.
</Define>

The right lens is **domain-driven design (DDD)** and specifically **bounded contexts**. A bounded context is a subsystem where a particular domain model is internally consistent and owns its own language.

For Meridian, the natural bounded contexts are:

- **Accounts** — user identities, balances, account state. The concept of an "account" here is a financial entity with a ledger balance; it is not the same as a "user profile" in a CMS.
- **Payments/Ledger** — double-entry bookkeeping, transfers, posting journal entries. (SQL & Databases in Go covers the SQL layer that underpins this; Part 3 goes deep on ledger design.)
- **Notifications** — delivery of events to users via email, push, or SMS. It knows *that* a payment happened, but it does not care *how* the ledger entry was constructed.

Each context owns its own data model. The `Transfer` type inside Meridian's Payments service looks different from whatever the Notifications service calls a "payment event" — and that's fine. They're in different bounded contexts; the same concept has a different representation in each.

**On the job:** You'll cut the wrong boundary at least once. The signal is when two services can't be deployed independently — a change in one always forces a change in the other. That's the boundary in the wrong place.

### The distributed monolith anti-pattern

You have a distributed monolith when services are split into separate deployables but remain tightly coupled:

<Define term="The Distributed Monolith (anti-pattern)">
A **distributed monolith** is what you get when you split a codebase into separate services but fail to separate their *coupling*. The services share a database, call each other synchronously across the board, or require coordinated deploys — so you have all the operational complexity of microservices with none of the independence. It is strictly worse than either a well-designed monolith or a well-designed microservices architecture. The tell: if you can't deploy service A without coordinating with service B, your boundary is wrong.
</Define>

<ConceptGrid cols={2}>
  <ConceptCard label="Shared database">Two services read and write the same tables. A schema change in one instantly breaks the other. You have all the coupling of a monolith with none of the simplicity.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Synchronous coupling chains">Service A calls B calls C calls D — synchronously. A is now blocked on D's latency and D's availability. Any single failure cascades to all callers.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Chatty interfaces">A business operation requires 10 sequential HTTP calls across services to complete. You haven't decomposed the domain — you've shredded a function across a network.</ConceptCard>
  <Gotcha>A shared database between services is not a microservices architecture. It is a monolith in a trenchcoat. Each service must own its data exclusively; other services ask for it through the API, never by querying the table directly.</Gotcha>
</ConceptGrid>

### Data ownership — one service, one schema

**Each service owns its data store. Full stop.** Other services don't get direct database access — they get an API.

```
meridian/accounts-service  ──── accounts_db  (schema: accounts, balances)
meridian/payments-service  ──── payments_db  (schema: transfers, journal_entries)
meridian/notifications-svc ──── notifications_db (schema: delivery_log, templates)
```

When Meridian's Payments service needs to check an account balance before posting a transfer, it calls `AccountsService.GetBalance` via the API. When the Payments service posts a `Transfer`, it emits a `TransferPosted` event that Notifications subscribes to. Nobody reaches across the schema boundary.

This has a real cost: **you lose ACID transactions that span service boundaries**. A `Transfer` that debits Account A and credits Account B — both owned by Meridian's Payments service — is fine. A transfer that requires updating both Payments *and* triggering a real-time Notifications delivery atomically is not — you need the **saga pattern** for that (Part 3: Transactions, Consistency and Sagas).

**On the job:** The urge to "just join the tables" across service boundaries is real, especially under deadline pressure. It always feels like a small shortcut. It always costs you later when one team renames a column.

Services need to talk. How they talk shapes every other property of the system — latency, availability, coupling, and failure modes.

<Define term="Inter-Service Communication (Sync vs. Async)" since="Messaging & Pub/Sub Foundations (Part 3)">
**Synchronous** communication (HTTP, gRPC) means the caller blocks until the callee responds — simple to reason about, but it ties the caller's availability directly to the callee's. If the callee is slow or down, the caller is affected. **Asynchronous** communication (message queues, events) means the caller publishes a message and returns immediately; the callee processes it later. This decouples availability — the caller keeps working even if the callee is down — but introduces eventual consistency: the caller can't immediately know the outcome. Right tool for the job: use sync for queries where the caller needs the answer before proceeding; use async for work that can happen later (notifications, audit logs, downstream projections).
</Define>

### Synchronous vs. asynchronous

The fundamental choice is whether the caller **waits for an answer** (synchronous) or **fires and moves on** (asynchronous).

<ConceptGrid cols={2}>
  <ConceptCard label="Synchronous (request/reply)">The caller blocks until the callee responds. Simple to reason about, easier to debug. Right for queries where you need the answer before proceeding. REST over HTTP and gRPC are the standard choices.</ConceptCard>
  <ConceptCard label="Asynchronous (message/event)">The caller publishes a message and returns immediately. The callee processes it later (maybe much later). Right for workflows that don't need an immediate answer — notifications, audit logs, downstream projections. Kafka, NATS, RabbitMQ. Deep treatment in Part 3.</ConceptCard>
</ConceptGrid>

### REST over HTTP — the pragmatic default

For most synchronous inter-service calls, plain HTTP/JSON (the same `net/http` you already know) is the right starting point. It's debuggable with `curl`, it's observable with standard tooling, and it adds zero new dependencies.

**On the job:** In Meridian's Payments service, every call to Accounts before posting a `Transfer` is a synchronous inter-service call. Getting the timeout and error handling wrong here means a slow Accounts pod takes Payments down with it.

Here is a minimal `AccountsClient` in Meridian's Payments service — a thin HTTP wrapper that calls the Accounts service to validate an account before accepting a transfer:

```go
package accounts

import (
	"context"
	"encoding/json"
	"fmt"
	"net/http"
	"time"
)

// Client calls Meridian's Accounts service over HTTP.
// The zero value is not useful — use NewClient.
type Client struct {
	base string
	http *http.Client
}

// AccountInfo is the subset of account state Payments actually needs.
type AccountInfo struct {
	ID       string `json:"id"`
	Currency string `json:"currency"`
	Active   bool   `json:"active"`
}

func NewClient(baseURL string, timeout time.Duration) *Client {
	return &Client{
		base: baseURL,
		http: &http.Client{Timeout: timeout},
	}
}

// GetAccount fetches Meridian account info by ID.
// ctx carries the deadline and cancellation signal from the caller.
func (c *Client) GetAccount(ctx context.Context, id string) (*AccountInfo, error) {
	req, err := http.NewRequestWithContext(
		ctx, http.MethodGet,
		fmt.Sprintf("%s/v1/accounts/%s", c.base, id),
		nil,
	)
	if err != nil {
		return nil, fmt.Errorf("accounts: build request: %w", err)
	}
	resp, err := c.http.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		return nil, fmt.Errorf("accounts: get account: %w", err)
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()
	if resp.StatusCode != http.StatusOK {
		return nil, fmt.Errorf("accounts: unexpected status %d", resp.StatusCode)
	}
	var info AccountInfo
	if err := json.NewDecoder(resp.Body).Decode(&info); err != nil {
		return nil, fmt.Errorf("accounts: decode response: %w", err)
	}
	return &info, nil
}
```

A few things to notice. `context.Context` is the first argument — this isn't ceremony, it's how the caller's deadline and cancellation propagate into the HTTP request. We use `http.NewRequestWithContext` (not `http.NewRequest`) so the context is wired to the transport. And we give `http.Client` an explicit `Timeout` — never trust a downstream service to respond in finite time.

Let's make this runnable with `httptest` so you can see it working without a real server:

### gRPC — when you need the next level

HTTP/JSON is fine for most inter-service calls. When you need **strong typing across service boundaries, streaming, or significantly better throughput**, gRPC is the answer: schema-first with Protocol Buffers, efficient binary wire format, generated clients in any language, and built-in deadline propagation.

gRPC gets full treatment in **Part 2, Chapter 17: gRPC and Service-to-Service Communication** (slug: `grpc-service-communication`). For now, the mental model: where HTTP/JSON lets you call across services without a shared schema, gRPC defines the contract in a `.proto` file and generates client/server code from it — the compiler catches API mismatches before they reach production. For Meridian's internal transfer RPC, gRPC is the right long-term answer; for getting started, HTTP/JSON is fine.

<Callout kind="note" title="Load balancers and service replicas — Deployment & Infra: CI/CD + Kubernetes">

In production you run *multiple replicas* of each service for availability and throughput. A **load balancer or reverse proxy** (nginx, Caddy, HAProxy, or a cloud LB) sits in front of those replicas and distributes traffic across them. It also handles TLS termination, health checking (dropping unhealthy replicas from rotation), and connection pooling. This is a deep topic — it gets full treatment in **Deployment & Infra: CI/CD + Kubernetes**. What you need to know now: Meridian's service clients call a stable address (the LB), not individual pod IPs.

</Callout>

### Service discovery basics

<Define term="Service Discovery" since="Deployment & Infra: CI/CD + Kubernetes (Part 1)">
**Service discovery** is how a service finds the network address of another service at runtime. In development, you hardcode `localhost:8081`. In production, services run as multiple replicas with dynamic IPs that change on every deploy and restart — so you can't hardcode them. The two common approaches: **DNS-based** (each service gets a stable DNS name backed by a load balancer, e.g. `accounts.meridian.svc.cluster.local` in Kubernetes) and **registry-based** (services register themselves at startup with a registry like Consul or etcd; clients query the registry). Kubernetes handles this automatically via its internal DNS. Without service discovery, a single replica restart breaks every caller that had its IP hardcoded.
</Define>

When Payments needs to find Accounts, it needs to know the Accounts service's address. In local development that's `localhost:8081`. In production it's more complex: services are deployed with dynamic IPs, auto-scaled, and restarted constantly.

**Service discovery** is the mechanism by which services find each other. The two common approaches:

- **DNS-based** — each service gets a DNS name (e.g., `accounts.meridian.svc.cluster.local` in Kubernetes). The DNS entry points at the load balancer. Simple, widely supported, sufficient for most systems.
- **Registry-based** — services register themselves on startup with a registry (Consul, etcd) and clients query the registry to find live instances. More dynamic, required for advanced routing.

For Kubernetes deployments (see Deployment & Infra: CI/CD + Kubernetes), the platform handles service discovery through its own DNS. For now: use a base URL injected via configuration (Configuration & Secrets Management covers this properly) and point it at a load balancer. No hardcoded IP addresses.

<UnderTheHood title="What crossing a network boundary actually costs">

An **in-process function call** in Go is essentially free: push arguments onto the stack, jump to the function, pop the return value. Time: nanoseconds. Failure modes: panics, which you control.

A **network call** across service boundaries is a different animal entirely. Here's what actually happens and what it costs:

**1. Serialization and deserialization.** The Go struct must be encoded to JSON (or protobuf) at the sender and decoded back to a struct at the receiver. For a simple `AccountInfo`, this is microseconds. For a large payload or a hot path, it compounds.

**2. TCP overhead.** A connection must be established (or reused from a pool), data split into packets, packets transmitted, acknowledgments received. Even on localhost, a minimal HTTP round-trip takes tens of microseconds. Over a datacenter network, 0.5–2ms is typical. Across availability zones, expect 1–5ms.

**3. OS context switches.** The kernel is involved for every send and receive. Goroutines block on I/O and yield their OS thread (the scheduler parks them and gives the thread to another goroutine — Concurrency: Goroutines, Channels & Select covers this), then get rescheduled when the response arrives.

**4. New failure modes.** A function call either returns or panics. A network call can: timeout, return a connection refused, return a 5xx, return a malformed response, or silently hang. You need **timeouts** (always), **retries with backoff** (carefully, idempotency first), and **circuit breakers** for sustained failures (Part 2, Chapter 11: Reliability Patterns).

**5. Observability.** A function call is visible in a stack trace. A network call crosses a process boundary — you need **distributed tracing** (correlation IDs, OpenTelemetry spans) to reconstruct the full call graph. Without it, debugging a slow request in a multi-service system is archaeology.

As of Go 1.26, the runtime's network poller handles async I/O efficiently — a goroutine blocked on a network read doesn't hold an OS thread. That's the scheduler doing its job (full GMP model: Part 2, Chapter 5). But no amount of scheduler cleverness eliminates the physics of network round trips. Design your service interactions to minimize synchronous network calls on the hot path.

**A note on error typing across service boundaries.** When Meridian's Payments service receives an error from the Accounts client, it often needs to distinguish a "timeout/unavailable" error (retry or degrade) from a "not found" error (fail fast, return 404 upstream). Go 1.26 introduces `errors.AsType[T]` — a generic convenience that replaces the two-step `errors.As` dance when you know the target type statically:

```go
// Go 1.26: errors.AsType[E error](err error) (E, bool) (new in 1.26)
// Walk the chain for a *url.Error to inspect whether the failure was a timeout.
if urlErr, ok := errors.AsType[*url.Error](err); ok && urlErr.Timeout() {
    // Accounts timed out — return 503, don't retry immediately.
    return nil, ErrAccountsUnavailable
}
```

The old `errors.As` with a typed target still works; `errors.AsType[T]` is a readability shortcut that avoids declaring a separate target variable. Deep coverage in Part 2, Chapter 2: Error Handling at Scale.

*This is a simplified model of what the runtime does — the actual poller and scheduler interact in ways detailed in Part 2, Chapter 5.*

</UnderTheHood>

## The Operational Cost

Here's where I stop being charming and start being honest with you. Microservices are genuinely powerful — at the right scale, with the right team, for the right reasons. They are also genuinely expensive to operate, and most of that cost is invisible until you're in it.

### Distributed tracing — debugging across process boundaries

In a monolith, a slow request shows up in one stack trace. In a multi-service system, that request touched five services, each with its own logs, its own trace, and its own wall clock. Without **distributed tracing**, you can't reconstruct what happened.

The standard today is **OpenTelemetry** — an instrumentation SDK you add to each service to emit **spans** (units of work with a start time, end time, and metadata). Spans are linked by a **trace ID** propagated in request headers (`traceparent`). The trace aggregator (Jaeger, Tempo, or your cloud provider's equivalent) stitches spans from multiple services into a single flame graph.

A trace for the transfer request above would show: time spent in the gateway → time spent in Payments before calling Accounts → time spent inside Accounts → time spent back in Payments posting the ledger entry → time spent in the response path. When Accounts is slow, it lights up in the trace immediately.

This is covered in depth in **Part 2, Chapter 10: Observability: Telemetry and Acting On It**. What you need to know now: plan for it from the first service split, not after you can't debug anything.

**On the job:** The first time you debug a multi-service issue without distributed tracing, you will lose two hours reconstructing a timeline from timestamped logs across three services. Add `traceparent` header propagation to every service client before you need it, not after.

### Partial failure — the hardest problem

<Define term="Partial Failure" since="Idempotency & Exactly-Once Semantics (Part 3)">
In a monolith, a call either returns a result or panics — you always know the outcome. In a distributed system, a third outcome exists: **partial failure**. The request left the caller, traveled the network, may have been processed by the callee, but the response was lost (network blip, pod restart, timeout). The caller doesn't know whether the operation happened. If it retries, did it double-charge? Partial failure is the single hardest problem in distributed systems, and it's why **idempotency keys** are mandatory for any mutating operation in financial systems — they let you safely retry without executing twice.
</Define>

In a monolith, a call either succeeds or it doesn't. In a distributed system, a call can **partially succeed** — the request reached the target, the target processed it, but the response was lost. You don't know if it ran. If you retry, did you double-charge?

This is why **idempotency keys** are not optional in financial systems, and why every mutating inter-service call that matters needs one. Deep treatment in **Part 3, Chapter 4: Idempotency and Exactly-Once Semantics**. The pattern: include a unique `Idempotency-Key` header on every write (every `POST /transfers` Meridian processes); the server deduplicates on that key so a safely-retried `Transfer` request is a no-op.

**On the job:** At Meridian, if a network blip causes the client to retry a `POST /transfers` without an idempotency key, the user gets charged twice. This is the most common and most expensive bug in payment systems. Idempotency keys are not optional.

### Partial failure cascading vs. containment

The fix is not complicated, but it requires discipline:

1. **Every outbound call has an explicit timeout.** Not the `http.DefaultClient` with no timeout — a `http.Client` with a `Timeout` set.
2. **Non-critical calls are async where possible.** Notifications don't need to block the transfer response. Enqueue them; deliver them separately.
3. **Circuit breakers** open after sustained failure, returning a fast error instead of queuing blocked goroutines (Part 2, Chapter 11: Reliability Patterns).

### Eventual consistency — your data will be stale sometimes

In a monolith with one database, a read after a write sees the write. In a distributed system where data is owned by separate services and communicated via events, a read by one service may not yet reflect a write that happened in another.

This is **eventual consistency** — the system *will* converge on the correct state, but not necessarily immediately. Some things to embrace:

- Model state transitions explicitly (e.g., a Meridian `Transfer` moves through `pending` → `settled` → `failed`) rather than treating everything as immediately consistent.
- Don't query Payments for data that should have been pushed from Accounts via an event — you might be reading stale state.
- Reconciliation jobs (a background worker that periodically validates that state is consistent across Meridian's services) are a necessary safety net, not an admission of defeat.

**On the job:** The first time a customer support ticket says "the payment shows posted but the notification never arrived," you will wish you had a reconciliation job running. Add it before you need it.

### Deployment and versioning complexity

With a monolith, "deploy" means one artifact, one version. With microservices, you have N services in production simultaneously, and during a rolling deploy, you have **two versions of one service running at the same time** alongside fixed versions of all other services.

This means:

- **API changes must be backward compatible** until you're certain all callers have upgraded. Never remove a field from a response payload before all consumers have stopped reading it.
- **Schema migrations must be expand-contract** — add the new column, backfill it, switch all callers, then drop the old. Same pattern as SQL & Databases in Go, now applied to service APIs.
- **Service versioning** (e.g., `/v1/accounts` vs. `/v2/accounts`) gives you a clean migration path, but you pay for maintaining two versions until the old one is retired.

package main

import (
	"context"
	"encoding/json"
	"fmt"
	"net/http"
	"time"
)

type accountResp struct {
	Active bool `json:"active"`
}

func FetchAccountStatus(ctx context.Context, baseURL, accountID string) (bool, error) {
	c := &http.Client{Timeout: 2 * time.Second}
	req, err := http.NewRequestWithContext(ctx, http.MethodGet,
		baseURL+"/v1/accounts/"+accountID, nil)
	if err != nil {
		return false, fmt.Errorf("build request: %w", err)

	resp, err := c.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		return false, fmt.Errorf("do request: %w", err)
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()
	if resp.StatusCode != http.StatusOK {
		return false, fmt.Errorf("unexpected status %d", resp.StatusCode)
	}
	var ar accountResp
	if err := json.NewDecoder(resp.Body).Decode(&ar); err != nil {
		return false, fmt.Errorf("decode: %w", err)
	}
	return ar.Active, nil
}

func main() {
	fmt.Println("FetchAccountStatus is defined and compiles.")
}

<Exercise n={3} title="Timeout fallback" level="medium">

Extend `FetchAccountStatus` to accept a fallback `bool` value. If the call fails for *any* reason (including a timeout), return the fallback and a nil error — log the error with `slog`. If the call succeeds, return the real value. This pattern keeps Payments running when Accounts is degraded.

<Solution>

```go
package main

import (
	"context"
	"encoding/json"
	"fmt"
	"log/slog"
	"net/http"
	"net/http/httptest"
	"time"
)

type accountResp struct {
	Active bool `json:"active"`
}

func FetchAccountStatusWithFallback(
	ctx context.Context,
	baseURL, accountID string,
	fallback bool,
) bool {
	c := &http.Client{Timeout: 500 * time.Millisecond}
	req, err := http.NewRequestWithContext(ctx, http.MethodGet,
		baseURL+"/v1/accounts/"+accountID, nil)
	if err != nil {
		slog.Warn("accounts: build request failed", "err", err)
		return fallback
	}
	resp, err := c.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		slog.Warn("accounts: request failed, using fallback",
			"account_id", accountID, "err", err)
		return fallback
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()
	if resp.StatusCode != http.StatusOK {
		slog.Warn("accounts: unexpected status, using fallback",
			"status", resp.StatusCode)
		return fallback
	}
	var ar accountResp
	if err := json.NewDecoder(resp.Body).Decode(&ar); err != nil {
		slog.Warn("accounts: decode failed, using fallback", "err", err)
		return fallback
	}
	return ar.Active
}

func main() {
	// Simulate Meridian's Accounts service being slow.
	srv := httptest.NewServer(http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
		time.Sleep(2 * time.Second) // longer than our 500ms timeout
		json.NewEncoder(w).Encode(accountResp{Active: true})
	}))
	defer srv.Close()

	active := FetchAccountStatusWithFallback(
		context.Background(), srv.URL, "acc_meridian_1",
		true, // assume active when Accounts is degraded
	)
	fmt.Println("active (with fallback):", active) // true — from fallback, not from Accounts
}
```

The fallback-to-true assumption makes sense for a read: if we can't verify the account right now, we optimistically proceed and let downstream validation catch it. For a debit, you'd want fallback-to-false (fail closed). The right default depends on the business risk.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="Spot the distributed monolith" level="medium">

Below are two service interaction designs for Meridian's payments system. Identify which one is a distributed monolith and explain why.

**Design A:** The Payments service embeds a `*sql.DB` for the `accounts_db` alongside its own `payments_db`. When posting a transfer, it runs a single SQL transaction that debits the accounts table (in `accounts_db`) and inserts a journal entry (in `payments_db`) atomically.

**Design B:** The Payments service calls `AccountsService.DebitAccount(ctx, id, amount)` via HTTP, which updates the accounts table. Payments then inserts a journal entry into its own `payments_db`. The two writes are *not* in a single database transaction; eventual consistency and the saga pattern (Part 3) handle failure.

<Solution>

**Design A is the distributed monolith.** Even though Payments and Accounts are conceptually separate services, Payments has direct `*sql.DB` access to `accounts_db`. This means:

- Any schema change to `accounts_db` by the Accounts team breaks the Payments service, silently and at runtime.
- The `accounts_db` has two owners — the Accounts service and the Payments service — violating the "one service, one database" rule.
- You cannot deploy or scale Payments independently of the accounts table schema.
- The "single SQL transaction spanning two databases" is actually two databases (unless you're using a distributed transaction protocol like 2PC, which is its own can of worms).

**Design B** trades the cross-DB ACID transaction for an API boundary and eventual consistency. That's a real cost (now you need idempotency keys and saga-style compensation on failure), but it's a cost that maintains genuine service independence. Each service owns its schema; failure modes are explicit; both services can be deployed independently.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} title="Service boundary analysis" level="hard">

A team proposes splitting the Meridian monolith into three services: `accounts-service`, `payments-service`, and `notifications-service`. They ask you to review the following two cross-cutting requirements and identify which ones create boundary problems:

1. The fraud-detection logic needs to read recent transaction history from `payments-service` *and* account KYC status from `accounts-service` in a single synchronous call before approving a payment. The proposed solution is a `fraud-service` that queries both databases directly for performance.

2. When a payment is posted, the `notifications-service` needs the payee's full name and email, which live in the `accounts-service` database. The proposed solution is a scheduled job in `notifications-service` that syncs a copy of the accounts table every 5 minutes.

<Solution>

**Requirement 1 — `fraud-service` querying both databases directly:**

This recreates the distributed monolith for a third service. `fraud-service` now owns nothing but has a hidden dependency on the schema of *two other services*. When either changes its schema, fraud-detection breaks.

*Corrected design:* `fraud-service` calls `AccountsService.GetKYCStatus(ctx, accountID)` and `PaymentsService.GetRecentTransactions(ctx, accountID, n)` via their APIs. Yes, this is two network calls instead of one query, but the boundaries stay clean. For performance, the fraud service can call both concurrently using goroutines and `errgroup`. If the latency is genuinely unacceptable, the right fix is caching at the fraud-service level (Caching & Redis), not database coupling.

**Requirement 2 — syncing the full accounts table into `notifications-service`:**

A periodic sync of a full table is a fragile, stale, and expensive coupling. The notifications service now has a 5-minute-stale snapshot of account data, and if the sync job fails, it drifts forever.

*Corrected design (preferred):* When Meridian's Payments service posts a `Transfer` and emits a `TransferPosted` event, it includes the payee's name and email in the event payload (or the information needed to look it up). Notifications subscribes to the event and uses the data embedded in it. The Accounts service is the single source of truth; the event carries what Notifications actually needs at the time of posting.

*Corrected design (synchronous alternative):* Notifications calls `AccountsService.GetContactInfo(ctx, accountID)` when processing a delivery. One targeted API call instead of a full table sync.

</Solution>

<Recap>

- A **monolith** compiles to one binary; service calls are function calls — zero network overhead, compiler-checked types, trivial rollback. Start here.
- A **modular monolith** packages domains into separate packages with clean boundaries but deploys as one unit. This is often the correct endgame, not a stepping stone.
- **Conway's Law**: your system architecture mirrors your team structure. Don't split before your team does.
- Split a service when you have genuine **independent scaling needs**, **multiple autonomous teams**, **fault isolation requirements**, or **compliance boundaries** — not before.
- **Bounded contexts** (DDD) are the right lens for service boundaries. Each service owns its own data store exclusively; other services use the API.
- A **distributed monolith** is the worst outcome: separate deployables that are still tightly coupled via shared databases or synchronous coupling chains.
- **gRPC** is the right tool when you need strong typing across service boundaries, streaming, or higher throughput. Full treatment in Part 2, Chapter 17: gRPC and Service-to-Service Communication.
- **Load balancers** sit in front of service replicas, handling TLS termination, health checking, and traffic distribution. Deep treatment in Deployment & Infra: CI/CD + Kubernetes.
- **Every outbound call needs an explicit timeout.** A slow downstream service must not cascade into an unavailable upstream. Add the timeout; add the fallback. `errors.AsType[T]` (new in Go 1.26) makes distinguishing timeout errors from "not found" errors cleaner.
- The operational tax: **distributed tracing**, **partial failure modes**, **eventual consistency**, **deployment versioning discipline**, and the platform to run it all. Pay it knowingly.

</Recap>
