<HeroCard label="Part 2 · Chapter 23" title="The Trouble with Distributed Systems">

On one machine, things work or they crash. A function returns or it doesn't; the clock reads what the clock reads; if the program is paused, everything is paused with it. The last six chapters spread the cinema over many machines, and each of those certainties is gone. A request can fail *partway*: the other side did the work, and you can't tell. A healthy machine can look dead. Two machines disagree about what time it is. A process can stop for seconds without knowing it stopped, then carry on as if nothing happened.

This chapter makes each of those happen to the cinema, on real processes, networks and clocks, and measures the damage. None of it is exotic: every failure here was produced with a proxy, a signal or a stopwatch. Then it builds the defence that works when you can't trust time or your own sense of it: fencing tokens.

Six stops. If you get lost, come back here:

1. **Partial failure.** An error doesn't mean it didn't happen.
2. **Is it dead?** Timeouts are the only failure detector, and they guess.
3. **Clocks.** Two machines never agree on the time, and last-writer-wins pays for it.
4. **Process pauses.** A lock holder that stops, resumes, and doesn't know.
5. **Fencing tokens.** Let the storage refuse the stale writer.
6. **The majority decides.** No node can trust its own view of itself.

</HeroCard>

## The lab bench

## Partial Failure

*Stop 1 of 6.*

### Step 1: charge a card, four ways

`payments` is the cinema's card processor: `POST /charge` records a charge and replies. `pay` charges one booking through Toxiproxy with a 1-second timeout. `partial.sh` charges four bookings: one with no fault, then one per fault, and then asks the payment service what it actually did:

```text
== no fault
cinema saw: 200 OK {"booking":"bk-1","charged":true} after 21ms
payment service: bk-1 WAS charged
== the request is lost on the way (nothing arrives)
cinema saw: error after 1.004s: Post "http://127.0.0.1:26403/charge?booking=bk-2": context deadline exceeded
payment service: bk-2 was NOT charged
== the reply is slow (3 s)
cinema saw: error after 1.001s: Post "http://127.0.0.1:26403/charge?booking=bk-3": context deadline exceeded
payment service: bk-3 WAS charged
== the connection drops before the reply
cinema saw: error after 5ms: Post "http://127.0.0.1:26403/charge?booking=bk-4": EOF
payment service: bk-4 WAS charged
```

Line the three failures up. **The cinema saw an error every time.** The payment service charged the card **twice**:

- **The request was lost on the way**: nothing reached the service; not charged.
- **The reply was slow**: the service charged at once and replied 3 s later; the cinema had stopped waiting at 1 s.
- **The connection dropped before the reply**: charged, then the connection closed; the cinema saw `EOF` after 5 ms.

### Step 2: what the cinema can do about it

The cinema cannot tell these apart from its side, and no amount of cleverness in the client changes that: the information isn't there. What it can do is make the question answerable:

- Send an **idempotency key** with the charge (the booking ID), so the service does it once however many times it's asked (Part 1 chapter 24).
- **Ask** afterwards: "did you charge bk-3?" (the payment service's `GET /charges` here), before retrying or telling the customer anything.
- **Never** report "payment failed" on a timeout. The honest message is "we're confirming your payment".

## Is It Dead?

*Stop 2 of 6.*

### Step 1: the only detector there is

A machine can't see into another. To decide whether the payment service is up, the cinema (or a load balancer, or a cluster manager) sends something and waits. No answer within the timeout: "dead". But a slow reply and a dead service look the same until the timeout ends, so the timeout decides how often a slow-but-healthy service is declared dead. `detector` probes the payment service 300 times per timeout, while Toxiproxy makes replies take 60 ms ± 60 ms (a busy but perfectly healthy service):

```text
# replies take 60 ms ± 60 ms (Toxiproxy jitter): a busy but healthy service
timeout 50ms   declared the live service dead 191 times in 300 (64%)
timeout 100ms  declared the live service dead  51 times in 300 (17%)
timeout 150ms  declared the live service dead   0 times in 300 (0%)
timeout 250ms  declared the live service dead   4 times in 300 (1%)
```

The service never went down. A **50 ms** timeout declared it dead **64%** of the time; **100 ms**, 17%; **150 ms**, never. And **250 ms**, well above the configured maximum of 120 ms, still called it dead **4 times**: real systems have a tail beyond what you think the worst case is (here, most likely the cost of opening new connections after earlier timeouts had closed them).

### Step 2: choosing the timeout

A short timeout detects real failures quickly and raises false alarms; a long one is accurate and slow. False alarms aren't free: declaring a healthy node dead triggers a failover (chapter 20: lost writes, two primaries) or moves its work elsewhere, adding load exactly when things are already slow. That's why systems measure the reply-time distribution and set the timeout above its tail (the idea behind adaptive detectors like the phi-accrual detector in Cassandra and Akka), and require several missed heartbeats before acting.

## Clocks

*Stop 3 of 6.*

### Step 1: how wrong is this clock?

Every machine has a quartz clock that drifts, corrected now and then over the network by NTP. How wrong can it be? The laptop this chapter was built on, asked three different time servers:

```text
$ sntp time.google.com
+105.313923 +/- 0.112237 time.google.com 216.239.35.4
$ sntp pool.ntp.org
+105.312979 +/- 0.074809 pool.ntp.org 103.97.91.223
$ sntp time.cloudflare.com
+105.318462 +/- 0.135261 time.cloudflare.com 162.159.200.123
```

**105 seconds slow**, confirmed by three independent servers (and by Google's HTTP `Date` header). Every timestamp this laptop wrote was 105 s in the past, and nothing complained.

Machines close to each other are closer, but never equal. `clockcheck` measures another machine's clock the way NTP does (Cristian's algorithm): note the local time, ask for theirs, note the local time again, and compare theirs with the midpoint, give or take half the round trip. Against the Docker VM on the same laptop:

```go noverify
// clockcheck estimates how far another machine's clock is from this one's
// (Cristian's algorithm, as NTP does in more detail): note our time, ask
// for theirs, note our time again; their clock minus the midpoint of our
// two readings is the offset, give or take half the round trip.
package main

import (
	"bufio"
	"flag"
	"fmt"
	"log"
	"net"
	"strconv"
	"strings"
	"time"
)

func main() {
	addr := flag.String("addr", "127.0.0.1:7123", "a timesrv")
	flag.Parse()
	c, err := net.Dial("tcp", *addr)
	if err != nil {
		log.Fatal(err)
	}
	r := bufio.NewReader(c)
	best := time.Duration(1 << 62)
	var bestOffset time.Duration
	for range 20 {
		t0 := time.Now()
		fmt.Fprintln(c, "time?")
		line, err := r.ReadString('\n')
		if err != nil {
			log.Fatal(err)
		}
		t1 := time.Now()
		theirs, _ := strconv.ParseInt(strings.TrimSpace(line), 10, 64)
		rtt := t1.Sub(t0)
		mid := t0.Add(rtt / 2).UnixNano()
		if rtt < best { // the shortest round trip gives the tightest estimate
			best, bestOffset = rtt, time.Duration(theirs-mid)
		}
	}
	fmt.Printf("their clock is %+v from ours (± %v, half the best round trip)\n", bestOffset.Round(time.Microsecond), (best / 2).Round(time.Microsecond))
}
```

```text
their clock is -901µs from ours (± 139µs, half the best round trip)
```

0.9 ms behind. Minutes later it was **1.145 ms** behind (the run below): the two clocks drift apart as you watch.

### Step 2: last-writer-wins, with real clocks

Chapter 20's quorum store resolved conflicts by "keep the write with the later timestamp". `lww` makes that concrete: Alice's writer on the Mac updates seat N, then tells Bob's writer in the container "your turn", and Bob updates it: Bob's write is **always** later. The store keeps whichever has the larger wall-clock timestamp:

```text
$ clockcheck     # the Docker VM's clock against this Mac's
their clock is -1.145ms from ours (± 116µs, half the best round trip)
$ lww first (on the Mac)   &   lww second (in a container)
200 rounds: bob's write reached Redis after alice's every time; 113 of them were discarded as older (56%)
bob's timestamps were earlier than alice's by a median of 514µs (range 11µs to 748µs)
```

Bob's write reached Redis second in all 200 rounds, and **113 were discarded as older** (56%), because Bob's clock was 1.1 ms behind and his write followed Alice's by less than that. An earlier run, with the clocks 0.9 ms apart, lost **186 of 200** (93%): the loss rate swings with the skew and the network's timing, and it's never zero while the skew exceeds the gap between causally ordered writes. No error was logged. A booking was simply replaced by the one before it.

### Step 3: what time is safe for

- **Durations on one machine**: use the **monotonic** clock. Go's `time.Now()` carries a monotonic reading, and `time.Since` uses it, so NTP stepping the wall clock doesn't make an elapsed time negative. (Stripping it with `t.Round(0)`, or serialising the time, loses that.)
- **Ordering events across machines**: don't use wall clocks. Use a counter every writer increments through the store (a version, a fencing token), or a **logical clock**: the lab builds a hybrid logical clock, which stays close to wall time but always orders "Alice's write, then Bob's" correctly.
- **When you must**: bound the uncertainty and wait it out. Google's Spanner does exactly that: its TrueTime API returns an interval, and a commit waits until the interval has passed before it's visible.

## Process Pauses

*Stop 4 of 6.*

### Step 1: a lock that expires while you hold it

Two box-office terminals try to finalize the sale of seat A7 at the same moment. Each takes a lock in Redis (`SET … NX PX 2000`: a lease that expires after 2 s, so a crashed holder can't block the seat forever), charges the card, then records the sale in Postgres.

```go noverify
// finalize sells seat A7 to one customer: take the seat's lock in Redis
// (a lease: it expires after 2 s), do the payment work, then record the
// sale in Postgres. With -fence, the lock comes with a fencing token and
// Postgres refuses a write whose token is older than one it has seen.
package main

import (
	"context"
	"flag"
	"fmt"
	"log"
	"time"

	"github.com/jackc/pgx/v5"
	"github.com/redis/go-redis/v9"
)

// acquire returns a new fencing token if it took the lock, 0 if the lock
// is held by someone else.
var acquire = redis.NewScript(`
if redis.call('SET', KEYS[1], ARGV[1], 'NX', 'PX', ARGV[2]) then
  return redis.call('INCR', KEYS[2])
end
return 0`)

func main() {
	customer := flag.String("customer", "alice", "")
	fence := flag.Bool("fence", false, "use fencing tokens")
	flag.Parse()
	ctx := context.Background()
	rdb := redis.NewClient(&redis.Options{Addr: "127.0.0.1:6390"})
	db, err := pgx.Connect(ctx, "postgres://postgres@127.0.0.1:55432/trouble")
	if err != nil {
		log.Fatal(err)
	}
	start := time.Now()
	say := func(format string, a ...any) {
		fmt.Printf("%-6s %5.2fs  %s\n", *customer, time.Since(start).Seconds(), fmt.Sprintf(format, a...))
	}

	// Take the lock (retrying while someone else has it), and in the same
	// atomic step get a fencing token: a counter that goes up by one every
	// time anyone takes this lock.
	var token int64
	for {
		t, err := acquire.Run(ctx, rdb, []string{"lock:inception:A7", "lock:inception:A7:token"}, *customer, 2000).Int64()
		if err != nil {
			log.Fatal(err)
		}
		if t > 0 {
			token = t
			break
		}
		time.Sleep(50 * time.Millisecond)
	}
	say("took the lock (lease 2 s), fencing token %d", token)

	time.Sleep(500 * time.Millisecond) // charge the card, etc.

	var tag interface{ RowsAffected() int64 }
	if *fence {
		tag, err = db.Exec(ctx, `UPDATE sales SET customer = $1, fence = $2
			WHERE movie = 'inception' AND seat = 'A7' AND fence < $2`, *customer, token)
	} else {
		tag, err = db.Exec(ctx, `UPDATE sales SET customer = $1 WHERE movie = 'inception' AND seat = 'A7'`, *customer)
	}
	if err != nil {
		log.Fatal(err)
	}
	if tag.RowsAffected() == 1 {
		say("wrote the sale: A7 is %s's", *customer)
	} else {
		say("the database refused the write: token %d is older than one it has already seen", token)
	}
}
```

</CodeWalk>

`pause.sh` has Alice take the lock and then **freezes her process** with SIGSTOP for about 3 s. That stands for any pause long enough to outlive a lease: a stop-the-world garbage collection, a virtual machine being migrated, a laptop lid closed, a process swapped out under memory pressure. While she's frozen, her lease expires; Bob takes the lock and makes the sale. Then Alice resumes:

```text
$ ./pause.sh
alice   0.01s  took the lock (lease 2 s), fencing token 1
        (alice is frozen)
bob     0.00s  took the lock (lease 2 s), fencing token 2
bob     0.51s  wrote the sale: A7 is bob's
        (alice resumes, unaware of the time that passed)
alice   3.59s  wrote the sale: A7 is alice's
final: A7 belongs to alice

$ ./pause.sh -fence
alice   0.00s  took the lock (lease 2 s), fencing token 1
        (alice is frozen)
bob     0.00s  took the lock (lease 2 s), fencing token 2
bob     0.51s  wrote the sale: A7 is bob's
        (alice resumes, unaware of the time that passed)
alice   3.57s  the database refused the write: token 1 is older than one it has already seen
final: A7 belongs to bob
```

Read the first run. Bob's times start from Bob's launch, 2.5 s into Alice's freeze. **Bob took the lock legitimately and sold A7 to Bob.** Then Alice resumed, with no idea that 3.5 s had passed, finished her "work", and **wrote A7 to Alice**, over Bob's sale. Final state: Alice's. Bob holds a confirmation for a seat that isn't his.

### Step 2: why checking the lease doesn't fix it

Chapter 13's sweeper checked its lease deadline before each run. Alice could do the same right before her `UPDATE`, and it would still fail: she could be paused **between** the check and the write. Any check the client makes of its own lease is a claim about the past by the time it acts on it. The protection has to live where the write lands.

## Fencing Tokens

*Stop 5 of 6.*

### Step 1: a number that only goes up

A **fencing token** is a number the lock service hands out with each lock, **larger every time**: Alice got 1, Bob got 2. Every write carries it, and the storage remembers the largest token it has accepted and **refuses any smaller one**. In `finalize`, the token comes from the same Lua script that takes the lock (`INCR` next to `SET NX`, so no two holders can get the same number), and the write is conditional:

```sql
UPDATE sales SET customer = $1, fence = $2
 WHERE movie = 'inception' AND seat = 'A7' AND fence < $2
```

The second run of `pause.sh` above, with `-fence`: Bob wrote with token 2; frozen Alice resumed and tried with token 1; **the database refused** (the `WHERE` matched no row), and A7 stayed Bob's. Alice finds out she lost the race, from the one component that knows.

### Step 2: what it requires

- **The storage must check.** Postgres here, with a condition in the `UPDATE`; object stores and some databases take a precondition (ETag, version) for the same purpose. A storage system that can't check can't be fenced.
- **The token must come from the lock service, atomically with the lock.** If it came from the client's clock, stop 3 would break it.
- **It doesn't stop the pause.** Alice still charged a card for a seat she didn't get: the payment side needs its own idempotency and a refund path. Fencing protects the shared state, not the world.

## The Majority Decides

*Stop 6 of 6.*

### Step 1: no node can trust itself

Every failure in this chapter shares a shape: a node acted on its own view of the world, and its view was wrong. The cinema thought the charge failed. The detector thought the service was dead. Bob's clock thought his write was older. Alice thought she still held the lock. Chapter 20 ended the same way: the old primary came back and believed it was still the primary.

A single node can't escape this: it can't tell a slow network from a dead peer, can't know it was paused, can't know its clock is 105 s wrong. So distributed systems don't let any single node decide facts that matter. They let a **majority** decide: a leader is the leader only while a majority acknowledges it; a write is committed only when a majority has it; a lock is held only while its holder can prove it with a token the majority's log issued. A node that's been partitioned away, paused or confused finds out when the majority rejects it.

### Step 2: what that costs, and where it's built

Majorities make the answers correct and make some of them slower or unavailable: a node cut off from the majority must stop accepting writes, even though it's running fine, because it can't prove it's still allowed. That's the trade chapter 24 builds: **consensus** (Raft, as in etcd), the algorithm that makes a majority agree on a sequence of decisions despite everything this chapter measured.

## Exercises

These use the `trouble` module and the containers from this chapter.

<Exercise n={1} title="Make the charge answerable" level="easy">

Add an idempotency key to `payments` and `pay`: the booking ID in an `Idempotency-Key` header; the service charges once per key and returns the original result on repeats. Rerun `partial.sh`, then retry each failed charge. How many charges does each booking end up with?

<Solution>

Exactly one each, for bk-2 through bk-4: bk-2 (never arrived) is charged on the retry; bk-3 and bk-4 (charged, reply lost) get the stored result back without a second charge. The service must store the key and result in the same transaction as the charge, or a crash between them reintroduces the problem, which is chapter 13's claim-then-act pattern again.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} title="Pick a timeout from the data" level="easy">

Change `detector` to record the reply-time distribution of 1,000 probes with no timeout under the same toxic, and choose a timeout from it. What percentile do you pick, and what false-alarm rate do you expect?

<Solution>

Record every latency, sort, and read p99, p99.9 and the maximum. A timeout a comfortable margin above p99.9 (with this toxic, about 150 ms plus the tail) gives a false-alarm rate around 0.1% per probe, and requiring three consecutive misses before acting brings the chance that a healthy service is declared dead to roughly one in a billion, if misses are independent. They often aren't (a GC pause delays several probes at once), which is why real detectors also look at how long it's been since the last success.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={3} title="Measure your own clocks" level="easy">

Run `sntp` against two NTP servers on your machine, and `clockcheck` against a container or a second machine. How far off are you? What would last-writer-wins do between your machines?

<Solution>

On a machine with working time sync, expect an NTP offset of a few milliseconds or less, and a few hundred microseconds to a few milliseconds between two machines on a LAN (more across data centres). This chapter's laptop was 105 s off because its time sync wasn't doing its job, which nothing noticed. Last-writer-wins between two machines discards a causally later write whenever the gap between the writes is smaller than the skew: with 1 ms of skew, any two writes by different machines within a millisecond of each other are ordered by luck.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={4} title="Fence the sweeper" level="medium">

Chapter 13's hold sweeper releases expired holds as the lease holder. Add fencing: where does the token come from, which writes carry it, and what must Redis check?

<Solution>

Take the lease and a token atomically (the same Lua pattern as `finalize`). Every write the sweeper makes carries the token: a Lua script that releases a hold first checks a per-film `sweeper:fence` key, refuses if the stored value is larger than the token, and otherwise records the token and deletes the hold, all in one script so Redis applies it atomically. A frozen old leader's release then fails, and the new leader's work isn't undone. Holds expire anyway, so here the damage fencing prevents is small; for a sweeper that charged or refunded, it wouldn't be.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={5} title="Refund the loser" level="hard">

With fencing, Alice's sale is refused, but her card was charged before she tried to write. Design the finalizer so that a refused write leads to a refund exactly once, even if Alice's process crashes right after the refusal.

<Solution>

Record intent before acting: when Alice takes the lock, write a `sale_attempts` row (booking, token, status 'charging') and charge with the attempt's ID as the idempotency key. After the fenced `UPDATE` is refused, mark the attempt 'lost' in the same transaction as a `refunds` outbox row (chapter 13's transactional outbox), and let a worker perform refunds with the attempt ID as the refund's idempotency key. If Alice crashes at any point, a reconciliation job finds attempts that are 'charging' but older than the lease and whose token isn't the seat's current fence, and marks them lost. Every step is either atomic or idempotent, so the refund happens exactly once.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## The Lab

<Lab
  title="A Clock That Keeps Order"
  archetype="build-it"
  difficulty="guided"
  points={120}
  hintCost={15}
  verify="self-check"
  expect="ALL CHECKS PASS"
  objective="Implement a hybrid logical clock: timestamps with a wall-clock part L and a counter C. Now must return a timestamp larger than anything the clock has returned or received (and plain wall time with C = 0 when the wall clock is ahead); Update, on receiving a message, must return something larger than the message's timestamp too. With it, last-writer-wins must keep the later write between two machines whose clocks disagree, which the chapter measured failing with raw wall clocks."
  starter={`package main

import "fmt"

// A hybrid logical clock (HLC) timestamps events so that if event B could
// have been caused by event A (A happened on B's machine earlier, or A's
// message had arrived), then B's timestamp is larger, even when the
// machines' wall clocks disagree, while staying close to wall-clock time.
//
// A timestamp is a wall-clock part L (nanoseconds) and a counter C that
// breaks ties when L can't move forward.

type Timestamp struct {
	L int64 // the largest wall-clock time this clock has seen
	C int64
}

func (a Timestamp) Less(b Timestamp) bool { return a.L < b.L || (a.L == b.L && a.C < b.C) }

type HLC struct {
	wall func() int64 // this machine's physical clock, in Unix nanoseconds
	last Timestamp
}

// Now timestamps a local event or a message about to be sent. It is one
// of the two methods you edit: the result must be larger than every
// timestamp this clock has returned or received, and equal to the wall
// clock (with C = 0) whenever the wall clock is ahead of both.
func (h *HLC) Now() Timestamp {
	return Timestamp{L: h.wall()}
}

// Update timestamps the receipt of a message sent at remote. It is the
// other method you edit: the result must be larger than remote, larger
// than everything this clock returned before, and follow the same rule
// about the wall clock.
func (h *HLC) Update(remote Timestamp) Timestamp {
	return h.Now()
}

// ── below this line: do not edit ──

// Machines' wall clocks in these checks are fixed functions of a shared
// "true time", so the skews are exact: the lesson is what the HLC does
// with a skew, not how skews arise (the chapter measured a real one).
func main() {
	allOK := true
	fail := func(format string, a ...any) { fmt.Printf("FAIL "+format+"\\n", a...); allOK = false }
	var trueTime int64 = 1_790_000_000_000_000_000

	// Check 1: the wall clock jumps backwards 5 ms (an NTP correction).
	wall := trueTime
	h := &HLC{wall: func() int64 { return wall }}
	a := h.Now()
	wall -= 5_000_000
	b := h.Now()
	c := h.Now()
	if !a.Less(b) || !b.Less(c) {
		fail("backward jump: %v, %v, %v are not increasing", a, b, c)
	}
	wall += 10_000_000
	if d := h.Now(); d.L != wall || d.C != 0 {
		fail("once the wall clock is ahead again, Now = %+v, want {L: wall clock, C: 0}", d)
	}

	// Check 2: the chapter's experiment. Alice's machine runs 1.1 ms ahead
	// of Bob's. Alice writes, tells Bob, and Bob writes 0.3 ms later.
	// Last-writer-wins by HLC timestamp must keep Bob's write, every time.
	lost := 0
	for i := range 100 {
		now := trueTime + int64(i)*1_000_000_000
		alice := &HLC{wall: func() int64 { return now + 1_100_000 }}
		bob := &HLC{wall: func() int64 { return now + 300_000 }}
		aliceWrite := alice.Now()
		bob.Update(aliceWrite) // the "your turn" message carries alice's timestamp
		bobWrite := bob.Now()
		if !aliceWrite.Less(bobWrite) {
			lost++
		}
	}
	if lost > 0 {
		fail("skewed writers: bob's later write lost %d times in 100 (with raw wall clocks it's 100 in 100)", lost)
	}

	// Check 3: a message from a clock far ahead can't drag this one
	// forward by more than it: L stays the largest time seen, no more.
	wall = trueTime
	h = &HLC{wall: func() int64 { return wall }}
	r := h.Update(Timestamp{L: trueTime + 2_000_000, C: 7})
	if r.L != trueTime+2_000_000 || r.C != 8 {
		fail("Update from a clock 2 ms ahead: got %+v, want {L: its L, C: its C + 1}", r)
	}
	if n := h.Now(); n.L != trueTime+2_000_000 || n.C != 9 {
		fail("Now right after it: got %+v, want the same L and C 9", n)
	}
	wall = trueTime + 3_000_000
	if n := h.Now(); n.L != wall || n.C != 0 {
		fail("once the wall clock passes it: got %+v, want {L: wall clock, C: 0}", n)
	}

	if allOK {
		fmt.Println("ALL CHECKS PASS")
	}
}`}
  verifier="Check 1: the wall clock jumps back 5 ms; Now must keep increasing, and return to wall time with C = 0 once the wall clock passes the last timestamp. Check 2: the chapter's experiment with exact skews (alice 1.1 ms ahead, bob writing 0.3 ms after her message): bob's write must win 100 times in 100. Check 3: a message from a clock 2 ms ahead gives {its L, its C + 1}, the next Now gives C + 2, and time moving past it resets C to 0. Prints ALL CHECKS PASS."
  hints={[
    "Now: read pt = wall(). If pt > last.L, the new timestamp is {pt, 0}; otherwise keep L and increment C. Store it as last and return it.",
    "Update: l = max(last.L, remote.L, pt). The counter depends on which of them l came from: both the local and remote L → max(last.C, remote.C) + 1; only the local → last.C + 1; only the remote → remote.C + 1; the wall clock → 0.",
    "The skew check needs no special code: bob.Update(aliceWrite) lifts bob's L to alice's, so his next Now is {alice's L, 1} or later, which is larger than hers."
  ]}
  flag="GO{order_by_cause_not_by_clock}"
>

This is the algorithm (Kulkarni et al., 2014) that CockroachDB and YugabyteDB use to timestamp transactions across machines whose clocks disagree by up to a configured maximum.

</Lab>

<Recap>

- **Partial failure**: three faults, three errors on the cinema's side, two cards charged (slow reply, dropped connection). An error from a remote call means unknown: ask, and make retries idempotent.
- **Failure detection is a guess**: a healthy service with 0–120 ms replies was declared dead 64% of the time at a 50 ms timeout, 17% at 100 ms, 0% at 150 ms, and still 4 times in 300 at 250 ms. Set timeouts above the measured tail; don't act on one miss.
- **Clocks disagree**: this laptop was 105 s behind NTP; its Docker VM was 0.9–1.1 ms behind it and drifting. Last-writer-wins by wall clock discarded 56–93% of causally later writes. Use monotonic time for durations and counters or logical clocks for order.
- **Process pauses**: a lock holder frozen past her 2 s lease resumed and overwrote the sale made by the new holder. No check a client makes of its own lease can prevent it.
- **Fencing tokens**: a strictly increasing number issued with the lock, checked by the storage (`WHERE fence < $2`): the stale write was refused and the sale stayed correct.
- **The majority decides**: every failure here was one node trusting its own view. Correct systems let a majority decide, which is chapter 24.

</Recap>
